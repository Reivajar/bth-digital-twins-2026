01

About the week

Most of you will never build a digital twin. You will commission one, write the requirements for it, sit in the meeting where a supplier explains what it can do, and decide whether that is true.

That is what this week prepares you for. We start from the concepts, hear the national vision, and then hear what one Swedish municipality, Helsingborg, actually has today. You then specify one function of a municipal city model and build a limited mockup of it, so that your specification rests on what the data allows rather than on what a rendering promises.

No programming background is assumed. You work on top of a prepared 3D city model, and AI-assisted coding is part of the method, not a shortcut around it.

By Friday you should be able to

  1. Tell a digital model, a digital shadow and a digital twin apart, and say which one a given system actually is.
  2. Describe digital twin practice in Swedish municipalities, and the gap between research, national vision and municipal reality.
  3. Identify who a function is for, which decision it supports, and which data it depends on.
  4. Write a requirements specification a municipality could build a procurement specification from.
  5. Build a limited mockup of one function or user journey, and report honestly on what it does not show.
  6. Judge a digital twin proposal critically: maintenance, ownership, and what the model leaves out.

03

Deliverable

A requirements specification (kravspecifikation), written as a municipal planner. This is the main deliverable. The mockup is a limited illustration of it.

It is a first draft of the requirements specification, written before procurement: what the municipality should ask a supplier for, and how it would check the delivery. It does not need to be a legally complete procurement document.

Purpose first, and be specific. Your twin, or the function you specify, must serve a purpose: support a decision, or answer an explicit exploratory question. "Show the city in 3D" is not a purpose. Name the place, the user, the decision or question, and the data. A specification that could have been written about any city, without this week, is not specific enough.

By group. The template is in the starter kit and under Documents.

The specification, 3–4 pages plus annexes

Be demanding here, above all about data: what the function needs, who holds it, how current it must be, and what is missing. Specify what the municipality should ask for, not only what you managed to build.

  1. Purpose and need. What the function is for: the decision it supports, or the question it explores if its purpose is exploratory. The planning problem, the place, and the named role whose work changes. Which other departments need the same function.
  2. Current state. What the municipality has today, classified with the card: model, shadow or twin.
  3. Requirements. Numbered "shall" statements in a table: ID, requirement, Must or Should, how a supplier's delivery would be checked, and whether the mockup shows it (yes, synthetic, no).
  4. Data. For each dataset: source, owner, how current, update cycle, licence, and what is missing.
  5. Organisation and maintenance. Who owns it, who updates it, how parallel versions are avoided.
  6. Not included. At least one thing deliberately left out, and why.
  7. Risks. What breaks in real use, including what the mockup could not do.
  8. Questions for the supplier. What you need answered before procuring.

Annex A is the mockup: a zipped folder that opens by double-click, 3–4 screenshots, and what is real and what is fake. Annex B is the build log and the AI comparison.

The mockup

A mockup, not a working product. It shows one functionality, or one journey of your user through the tool: what they see, what they click, what they get back. It can be entirely fake: hard-coded results, invented data, screens that only pretend to calculate. That is fine, as long as you say so.

Accepted
One user journey

A few screens or states that walk through one task, from question to answer. Results can be faked.

Accepted
One working function

A single function that runs on real or synthetic data in the starter kit.

Accepted if documented
Does not work

Show how far you got and document precisely where and why it stopped.

Never cut the specification to fit the mockup. An ambitious specification with a limited mockup scores better than a modest specification that the mockup fully shows. In the requirements table, mark what the mockup shows (yes, faked, no). A Must requirement the mockup cannot show goes in Risks, not in the bin.

These are not a list to choose from. They are deliberately basic examples to try on the starter kit while you learn to vibecode.

Use them on Tuesday afternoon and Wednesday morning to get a feel for the kit, the data and the AI tool. Your group's project is your own function, defined on Wednesday: it can grow out of one of these, combine them, or go somewhere else entirely, as long as it serves a clear purpose for a named user. Helsingborg is the default case, because we hear from the people who run its model and the kit is built for it.

Ideas for going further

Directions for your own function once you know the kit. They are prompts to think with, not a menu, and they take more than the basic examples, so scope them down by Thursday's checkpoint if needed. Each still needs a named user and a purpose: a decision it supports, or a question it explores.

Microsimulation of one square4
  • People on Stortorget. Dots walk across Helsingborg's main square between entrances, avoiding buildings and each other. User: a planner redesigning the square for an event. The movement is invented. Your specification says what data would calibrate it (counts, mobile-network data) and who owns it.
  • Sun and shade. Where the square is in shade at 15:00 in March, June and September, from the sun's position and building heights. User: a plan architect placing benches or a café terrace.Check the heights of the buildings around it. The kit guesses many of them.
  • Street canyons and wind. Height-to-width ratio of the streets around the square as a stand-in for wind and ventilation. User: an environmental planner screening a new building.An indicator, not a wind simulation. Saying so is part of the grade.
  • Who can reach it. A gravity model of trips to the square from the surrounding blocks, with population from SCB. User: a traffic planner choosing where to put cycle parking.Synthetic trips, declared as such.
Unconventional and social data4
  • What people photograph. Street-level and geotagged images, such as Mapillary, show which streets people document and how places look today. User: a planner checking whether the model still matches the street.Who takes photos is not who uses the city. That bias is a finding.
  • What is there, and when it is open. Overture's places layer lists shops, cafés and services with categories. Map which streets are alive at 20:00. User: a planner working on a town-centre strategy.Coverage of small and informal businesses is patchy.
  • The map's own history. OpenStreetMap keeps every edit. Where were buildings last edited? That is a real, public version of option B's invented "last checked" year. User: the GIS engineer who maintains the model.A volunteer edit is not a survey. Say what it does and does not prove.
  • What residents say. Comments from a consultation (medborgardialog), placed on the map and grouped by theme. User: a planner preparing a detailed plan's consultation report.Use comments you write yourselves or ones published openly, never personal data.
Simulate what is missing5
  • Who lives here. A synthetic population per building, from floor area and an assumed area per person, checked against SCB's grid totals. User: a planner estimating school or care demand for a new district.State the assumptions, then show how much the result moves when you change them.
  • How much energy. Rough heating demand per building from size, age class and use. User: an energy or climate strategist choosing where to start renovation.Age and use are mostly unknown in the kit. Inventing them is fine if you say how.
  • How loud. Noise falling off with distance from busy roads, coloured on façades. User: a plan architect placing housing near a main road.A real noise study follows national methods. Yours is a screening sketch.
  • Replay a live day. Record a day of real data once, such as bus positions from Trafiklab or hourly air quality in Helsingborg, save it as a file, and play it back on the map as if it were live. User: an operations or environment officer asking what a live feed would change in their work.This shows the data flow that makes a twin a twin, without depending on Wi-Fi or an API key during the presentation. Your specification says who would keep the feed running.
  • Two futures side by side. The same area in 2050 and 2100, or with and without a densification scenario. User: a political committee comparing options.Scenarios are where twins are most often oversold. Show the uncertainty, not one picture.
Somewhere else, or something else3
  • A different place. Your hometown, a municipality you have worked in, or a small one that will never have a twin. What would a model there need to do first? Overture covers all of Sweden, so the AI can help you swap in another area.See the three conditions below.
  • A twin for dialogue, not analysis. A screen residents or developers use in a meeting: pin a comment, compare two massings, vote. User: the planner running the meeting.Here the purpose is the conversation, not the calculation. Say how you would know it worked.
  • Your own question. If you think something important is missing from the data, from the examples or from this page, build a sketch of it with synthetic data and make the case in your specification.An exploratory purpose is a purpose, as long as the question is stated.

Whatever you choose, invented or simulated data looks more certain than it is. The honesty box matters most here.

Working on another municipality

You may choose another Swedish municipality instead of Helsingborg, if it serves your function better or you know it from work. Malmö (urban regeneration, climate adaptation, cycling), Göteborg (port, freight, climate resilience), Stockholm (metropolitan scale, multimodal transport) and Lund (university–city collaboration) are all reasonable. So is a smaller municipality you know.

Three conditions, agreed with the teacher by Wednesday 11:00:

  • You can name the user role in that municipality and the purpose the function serves: a decision or an exploratory question.
  • The data you need is openly available. Check with data and tools below.
  • You accept the cost. The starter kit contains only Helsingborg, so you load your own buildings, which takes time out of a build of about six hours. Synthetic data is still accepted if declared.

06

Using AI

AI-assisted coding is how you build the mockup, and it is allowed and expected. What is assessed is your judgment around it. AI tool TBC

Keep data out

Never paste the data files into a chat. Paste the code plus three sample buildings.

Save numbered copies

Save v1.html, v2.html every time it works. This is your undo button.

Rotate the keyboard

Take turns typing the prompts. The build log names who wrote each one.

Anchor the specification

Use what only you have: Tuesday's session, the checkpoint, and what your mockup could not do.

Log as much as you can. The build log is Annex B and part of the grade, and it is far easier to write as you go than on Friday. Keep one shared document per group and add to it every time you work:

  • Prompts: what you asked, in full, and who typed it.
  • Answers and edits: what the AI returned, what you kept, what you changed by hand, and which numbered copy it became.
  • Data: every dataset you used or tried, where it came from, when you downloaded it, and what you changed in it.
  • Failures: what broke, the error message, and how you got back.
  • Decisions: what you chose not to build, and why.

Screenshots count. A rough log with everything in it beats a tidy one written afterwards.

Data and tools

A municipal twin is rarely one platform. It is a federation of datasets owned by different organisations at different levels.

A curated starting point, not a complete list. Each entry says what it offers and what it takes to get it. KIT marks data that can go straight into the starter kit as a downloaded file. Live feeds and APIs usually cannot, because the kit runs without a server: describe them in your specification instead.

Want to use a live API in your mockup? Ask Javier first. Some need a key, some refuse requests from a page opened as a file, and we can check together whether yours works before you build on it.

Ask of every dataset

  1. Who owns it, and who updates it?
  2. When was it last updated?
  3. How do you get it: download, account, API key? Under what licence?
  4. Is it authoritative for this decision?
  5. Does it support a planning decision?

  • SMHI: future mean sea level per coastal municipality, by scenario and decade (2030–2150), in RH 2000. Also open data and an observations API.KITThe projections are what option A needs. Mean level only: storm surges come on top.
  • SCB: open geodata. Population by grid, DeSO and RegSO areas, built-up areas.KITThe base for any synthetic population or gravity model.
  • Trafikverket: open traffic API. Road works, disruptions, road conditions, rail, ferries, road weather. Real time.Free API key. Describe in the specification rather than build.

  • Trafiklab: national public transport data, including Skånetrafiken. GTFS timetables and stops, plus real-time feeds.KITTimetables and stops work as a file. Real-time feeds need a key and a server.
Global and large-scale4
  • Overture Maps: overturemaps.org. Buildings, transport, places.KITThe starter kit's buildings come from here. 56% of them have no height and are set to 6 m. In central Helsingborg the city's own model shows they are closer to 19 m.
  • OpenStreetMap: openstreetmap.org. Streets, paths, cycleways, stops, amenities, parks, land use.KITAlready inside Overture. Heights mostly missing.
  • Copernicus: Data Space. Satellite imagery, elevation, land cover.KITThe kit's ground elevation is the 30 m Copernicus model: fine for a first look, too coarse for a flood assessment.
  • GHSL, Global Human Settlement Layer: EU Joint Research Centre. Built-up area, population and degree of urbanisation on global grids, 1975–2030.KITFor large-scale urban analytics: how Helsingborg's built-up area grew, or how it compares with other cities. Grid cells, not buildings.
Real-time and activity data5
  • Before you start: ask Javier if you want to use any of these live. Recording a day and replaying it is the safe alternative.
  • Air quality, Helsingborg: hourly NO₂, SO₂, ozone, PM10 and PM2.5 through the city's portal.The only live municipal feed on this list.
  • Weather and sea level, SMHI: the observations API, near real time.
  • Public transport, Trafiklab: GTFS-Realtime vehicle positions and delays.
  • Telraam: citizen-run traffic counters counting pedestrians, cyclists, cars and heavy vehicles, with an open API.Coverage depends on where volunteers have placed a counter.
  • Telia Crowd Insights: people-movement statistics from the mobile network.This is how a municipality would know how many people visit a square. It is commercial and built on personal data, so it cannot go in your mockup, but your specification can require it: who buys it, at what resolution, and under which GDPR basis.
Synthetic data and simulation5
  • Prompting for synthetic data. Ask the AI to generate data inside the page, not a separate file, and to label it. For example: "Create 2,000 synthetic trips between the kit's buildings using a gravity model: trips between two places grow with their sizes (use footprint area × height) and fall with distance squared. Show them as lines whose thickness is the number of trips. Label everything 'synthetic'."Synthetic is fine if you declare it. The finding is which real data would replace it, and who holds that data.
  • SunCalc: sun position in JavaScript (BSD-2-Clause).Small enough to save into the kit. Enough for "where is the sun at 15:00 on 21 March", and rough shadow lengths from building heights.
  • UMEP for QGIS: urban climate tools: solar radiation, shadows, thermal comfort, sky view.Open source. What a municipality's analyst would use for the real version of a solar or heat study.
  • Eclipse SUMO: traffic microsimulation, open source.Real traffic and pedestrian simulation, but an installed program. Name it in the specification as the tool behind a full version.
  • Wind and street canyons. Proper wind simulation needs CFD software and days of set-up. In the kit you can compute an indicator instead, such as the ratio of building height to street width.Say clearly in the honesty box that it is an indicator, not a simulation.

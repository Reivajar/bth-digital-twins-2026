01

About the week

Most of you will never build a digital twin. You will commission one, write the requirements for it, sit in the meeting where a supplier explains what it can do, and decide whether that is true.

That is what this week prepares you for. We start from the concepts, hear the national vision, and then hear what one Swedish municipality, Helsingborg, actually has today. You then specify one function of a municipal city model and build a limited mockup of it, so that your specification rests on what the data allows rather than on what a rendering promises.

No programming background is assumed. You work on top of a prepared 3D city model, and AI-assisted coding is part of the method, not a shortcut around it.

By Friday you should be able to

  1. Tell a digital model, a digital shadow and a digital twin apart, and say which one a given system actually is.
  2. Describe digital twin practice in Swedish municipalities, and the gap between research, national vision and municipal reality.
  3. Identify who a function is for, which decision it supports, and which data it depends on.
  4. Write a requirements specification a municipality could build a procurement specification from.
  5. Build a limited mockup of one function or user journey, and report honestly on what it does not show.
  6. Judge a digital twin proposal critically: maintenance, ownership, and what the model leaves out.

Friday 09:15 · C533
The presentation

In person, 12 minutes per group: about 8 minutes showing and telling what you built (or failed to build), then about 4 minutes of questions and changeover.

Where and when: in Canvas, by Friday 13 November at 17:00. Replace NN with your group number.

The specification is the main deliverable. The mockup is evidence for it: it may be partly or entirely faked, as long as you say so. Your function must serve a purpose: support a decision, or answer an explicit exploratory question. "Show the city in 3D" is not a purpose. Name the place, the user, the decision or question, and the data.

The specification: at most 8 pages, plus annexes

A first draft written before procurement: what the municipality should ask a supplier for, and how it would check the delivery. It does not need to be a legally complete procurement document. Use the template in the starter kit or under Documents. Be demanding, above all about data: specify what the municipality should ask for, not only what you managed to build.

  1. Purpose and need. What the function is for: the decision it supports, or the question it explores. The planning problem, the place, and the named role whose work changes. Which other departments need the same function.
  2. Current state. What the municipality has today, and whether that is a model, a shadow or a twin.
  3. Requirements. Numbered "shall" statements in a table: ID, requirement, Must or Should, how a supplier's delivery would be checked, and whether the mockup shows it (yes, synthetic, no).
  4. Data. For each dataset: source, owner, how current, update cycle, licence, and what is missing.
  5. Organisation and maintenance. Who owns it, who updates it, how parallel versions are avoided.
  6. Not included. At least one thing deliberately left out, and why.
  7. Risks. What breaks in real use, including what the mockup could not do.
  8. Open questions and who to ask. Three things you would need answered before this could go ahead, and who you would ask each one: a data owner, a colleague in another department, legal, or a supplier.

Annex A: 3–4 screenshots of the mockup, and one paragraph on what is real, what is synthetic and what the mockup does not show. Annex B: the build log (see Using AI). The code itself goes in the zip, not in the PDF.

The mockup

A mockup, not a working product. It shows one functionality, or one journey of your user through the tool: what they see, what they click, what they get back. It can be entirely fake: hard-coded results, invented data, screens that only pretend to calculate. That is fine, as long as you say so in Annex A.

It does not have to be a 3D model or a map. A digital twin can also be a dashboard, or a screen where people and systems exchange data. Choose what your user needs.

Accepted
One user journey

A few screens or states that walk through one task, from question to answer. Results can be faked.

Accepted
One working function

A single function that runs on real or synthetic data in the starter kit.

Accepted if documented
Does not work

Show how far you got and document precisely where and why it stopped.

Hand in the complete code in the zip, so the mockup can be opened. The code is not graded on its quality as software.

Never cut the specification to fit the mockup. An ambitious specification with a limited mockup scores better than a modest specification that the mockup fully shows. A Must requirement the mockup cannot show goes in Risks, not in the bin.

Starting examples

Practice examples for Tuesday afternoon: deliberately basic, to learn the starter kit and the AI assistant.

Use them on Tuesday afternoon to get a feel for the kit, the data and the AI tool. Your group's project is your own function, agreed on Wednesday by 14:00. It can grow out of one of these examples, or not, as long as it serves a clear purpose for a named user. Helsingborg is the default case, because we hear from the people who run its model and the kit is built for it.

Starting points, not the destination. Each example has a ready-made prompt in the kit's docs/PROMPTS.md. Expect to go further than any of them.

Ideas for going further (optional)

If you want inspiration for your own function. Each takes more than the basic examples, so scope it down by Thursday's checkpoint. Each still needs a named user and a purpose.

Eight ideas8
  • People on Stortorget. Dots walk across Helsingborg's main square between entrances, avoiding buildings and each other. User: a planner redesigning the square for an event. The movement is invented. Your specification says what data would calibrate it (counts, mobile-network data) and who owns it.
  • Sun and shade. Where the square is in shade at 15:00 in March, June and September. User: a plan architect placing benches or a café terrace.The kit guesses many building heights. Check them.
  • Who lives here. A synthetic population per building, from floor area and an assumed area per person, checked against SCB's grid totals. User: a planner estimating school or care demand.State the assumptions, then show how much the result moves when you change them.
  • Replay a live day. Record a day of real data once (bus positions from Trafiklab, or hourly air quality in Helsingborg) and play it back as if it were live. User: an operations or environment officer.Shows the data flow that makes a twin a twin, without needing Wi-Fi during the presentation.
  • A data dashboard, no map. A screen where a department enters and reads the data it shares with another, such as building permits feeding the 3D model team. User: the GIS engineer who maintains the model.The twin as a data exchange rather than a picture.
  • What residents say. Comments from a consultation (medborgardialog), placed on the map and grouped by theme. User: a planner preparing a consultation report.Use comments you write yourselves or ones published openly, never personal data.
  • Two futures side by side. The same area in 2050 and 2100, or with and without a densification scenario. User: a political committee comparing options.Show the uncertainty, not one picture.
  • Your own question. If something important is missing from the data or from this page, build a sketch of it with synthetic data and make the case in your specification.An exploratory purpose is a purpose, as long as the question is stated.

Invented or simulated data looks more certain than it is. Say clearly in Annex A what is real and what is not.

Working on another municipality

You may choose another Swedish municipality instead of Helsingborg, if it serves your function better or you know it from work. Malmö (urban regeneration, climate adaptation, cycling), Göteborg (port, freight, climate resilience), Stockholm (metropolitan scale, multimodal transport) and Lund (university–city collaboration) are all reasonable. So is a smaller municipality you know.

Three conditions, agreed with the teacher by Wednesday 14:00:

  • You can name the user role in that municipality and the purpose the function serves: a decision or an exploratory question.
  • The data you need is openly available. Check with data and tools below.
  • You accept the cost. The starter kit contains only Helsingborg, so you load your own buildings, which takes time out of a build of about six hours. Synthetic data is still accepted if declared.

Using AI

AI-assisted coding is how you build the mockup, and it is allowed and expected. Any AI assistant works: BTH's Copilot licence is enough, and Claude or another service is fine too.

Keep data out

Never paste the data files into a chat. Paste the code plus three sample buildings.

Save numbered copies

Save v1.html, v2.html every time it works. This is your undo button.

Rotate the keyboard

Take turns typing the prompts. The build log names who wrote each one.

Anchor the specification

Use what only you have: Tuesday's session, the checkpoint, and what your mockup could not do.

Log as much as you can. The build log is Annex B of your PDF, and it is far easier to write as you go than on Friday. Keep one shared document per group and add to it every time you work:

  • Prompts: what you asked, in full, and who typed it.
  • Answers and edits: what the AI returned, what you kept, what you changed by hand, and which numbered copy it became.
  • Data: every dataset you used or tried, where it came from, when you downloaded it, and what you changed in it.
  • Failures: what broke, the error message, and how you got back.
  • Decisions: what you chose not to build, and why.

Screenshots count. A rough log with everything in it beats a tidy one written afterwards.

The minimum is one text from each of the three categories: Concept, Practice and Critique. All are free to read. Read them before Wednesday's seminar, in this order. Each card has a twin on its back: flip it if the other version suits you better. Either side counts as the reading.

Batty, M. (2024). Digital twins in city planning. Nature Computational Science, 4(3), 192–199. Free author version

Short, written by an urbanist for planners. Read it for where twins help planning and where the idea breaks down.

Wright, L., & Davidson, S. (2020). How to tell the difference between a model and a digital twin. Advanced Modeling and Simulation in Engineering Sciences, 7, 13.

You gain a sharp test: a twin is defined by the question it answers. That is the purpose we grade you on. You lose the city: the examples come from engineering.

2 · Practice

Kruse, E. N., Nielsen, T. T., & de Neergaard, M. (2026). From epistemic promises to real world practices: The ongoing becoming of 3D models and urban digital twins in municipal planning. Computational Urban Science, 6, 62.

Two Swedish and one Danish municipality, followed over time. Skim the theory and read the cases.

2 · Practice, easier

Herzog, R. H. (2025). Towards hyperreal planning? Surveying practical uses of digital models and simulations in Hamburg's public administration. European Planning Studies, 34(3), 603–623.

You gain plain planning language and a survey of what one administration actually uses. You lose the Swedish cases, which tie in with Tuesday.

3 · Critique

de Wilde de Ligny, S., van Geldere, S., Schäfer, M. T., & Meijer, A. J. (2025). There is no such thing as a digital twin. New Media & Society, 27(8), 4420–4442.

Three years inside Dutch local government. The theory is dense: go straight to the findings.

3 · Critique, easier

Mattern, S. (2017). A city is not a computer. Places Journal.

You gain a readable essay written for designers. You lose the fieldwork: it argues about smart cities in general, while Wednesday asks what governments actually did.

Data and tools

A municipal twin is rarely one platform. It is a federation of datasets owned by different organisations at different levels.

Optional reference. Use it when you need data, not as reading. A curated starting point, not a complete list. Each entry says what it offers and what it takes to get it. KIT marks data that can go straight into the starter kit as a downloaded file. Live feeds and APIs usually cannot, because the kit runs without a server: describe them in your specification instead.

Want to use a live API in your mockup? Ask Javier first. Some need a key, some refuse requests from a page opened as a file, and we can check together whether yours works before you build on it.

Ask of every dataset

  1. Who owns it, and who updates it?
  2. When was it last updated?
  3. How do you get it: download, account, API key? Under what licence?
  4. Is it authoritative for this decision?
  5. Does it support a planning decision?

  • Lantmäteriet: open geodata. Building footprints, addresses, the national elevation model (1–2 m), orthophotos, hydrography, boundaries.KITDownloads go through Geotorget and need an account, which can take days to approve. Apply early if you want it.
  • SMHI: future mean sea level per coastal municipality, by scenario and decade (2030–2150), in RH 2000. Also open data and an observations API.KITThe projections are what option A needs. Mean level only: storm surges come on top.
  • SCB: open geodata. Population by grid, DeSO and RegSO areas, built-up areas.KITThe base for any synthetic population or gravity model.
  • Trafikverket: open traffic API. Road works, disruptions, road conditions, rail, ferries, road weather. Real time.Free API key. Describe in the specification rather than build.
  • Geodataportalen: national geodata catalogue, run by Lantmäteriet.A search tool, not a download. Terms depend on each provider.
  • Boverket: digital detailed plans.For a specification that links a function to plan decisions. How many of a municipality's plans are digital varies.
  • Sveriges dataportal: dataportal.se, the national catalogue of open data from public bodies.Good for checking whether a municipality publishes what you need.
Municipal and regional sources5
  • Helsingborg: open data portal. 3D buildings, LiDAR (2019), orthophotos, base map, addresses, contours, delivery data, and hourly air quality (Luften i Helsingborg).The 3D buildings are SketchUp tiles dated 2014, geometry only, no attributes. Accurate, maintained by hand, not queryable: a good example for option B. The city is reviewing its open data, so some sets may be missing for a while.
  • Malmö: open data portal.For groups working on Malmö, including the Södra Förstadsgatan reference project.
  • Göteborg: open data from Stadsmiljöförvaltningen.Needs a free account and a key.
  • Stockholm: traffic and street data (WMS, WFS, OGC API, parking).Free to reuse unless stated otherwise.
  • Trafiklab: national public transport data, including Skånetrafiken. GTFS timetables and stops, plus real-time feeds.KITTimetables and stops work as a file. Real-time feeds need a key and a server.
Global and large-scale4
  • Overture Maps: overturemaps.org. Buildings, transport, places.KITThe starter kit's buildings come from here. 56% of them have no height and are set to 6 m. In central Helsingborg the city's own model shows they are closer to 19 m.
  • OpenStreetMap: openstreetmap.org. Streets, paths, cycleways, stops, amenities, parks, land use.KITAlready inside Overture. Heights mostly missing.
  • Copernicus: Data Space. Satellite imagery, elevation, land cover.KITThe kit's ground elevation is the 30 m Copernicus model: fine for a first look, too coarse for a flood assessment.
  • GHSL, Global Human Settlement Layer: EU Joint Research Centre. Built-up area, population and degree of urbanisation on global grids, 1975–2030.KITFor large-scale urban analytics: how Helsingborg's built-up area grew, or how it compares with other cities. Grid cells, not buildings.
Real-time and activity data5
  • Before you start: ask Javier if you want to use any of these live. Recording a day and replaying it is the safe alternative.
  • Air quality, Helsingborg: hourly NO₂, SO₂, ozone, PM10 and PM2.5 through the city's portal.The only live municipal feed on this list.
  • Weather and sea level, SMHI: the observations API, near real time.
  • Public transport, Trafiklab: GTFS-Realtime vehicle positions and delays.
  • Telraam: citizen-run traffic counters counting pedestrians, cyclists, cars and heavy vehicles, with an open API.Coverage depends on where volunteers have placed a counter.
  • Telia Crowd Insights: people-movement statistics from the mobile network.This is how a municipality would know how many people visit a square. It is commercial and built on personal data, so it cannot go in your mockup, but your specification can require it: who buys it, at what resolution, and under which GDPR basis.
Synthetic data and simulation5
  • Prompting for synthetic data. Ask the AI to generate data inside the page, not a separate file, and to label it. For example: "Create 2,000 synthetic trips between the kit's buildings using a gravity model: trips between two places grow with their sizes (use footprint area × height) and fall with distance squared. Show them as lines whose thickness is the number of trips. Label everything 'synthetic'."Synthetic is fine if you declare it. The finding is which real data would replace it, and who holds that data.
  • SunCalc: sun position in JavaScript (BSD-2-Clause).Small enough to save into the kit. Enough for "where is the sun at 15:00 on 21 March", and rough shadow lengths from building heights.
  • UMEP for QGIS: urban climate tools: solar radiation, shadows, thermal comfort, sky view.Open source. What a municipality's analyst would use for the real version of a solar or heat study.
  • Eclipse SUMO: traffic microsimulation, open source.Real traffic and pedestrian simulation, but an installed program. Name it in the specification as the tool behind a full version.
  • Wind and street canyons. Proper wind simulation needs CFD software and days of set-up. In the kit you can compute an indicator instead, such as the ratio of building height to street width.Say clearly in Annex A that it is an indicator, not a simulation.
